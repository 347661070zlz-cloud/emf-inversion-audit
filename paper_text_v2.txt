ABSTRACT Smartphone-based inversion of electric field strength is attractive for crowdsourced electro-
magnetic environment monitoring, yet reported mean absolute errors (MAE) around 1 dB are commonly
obtained with a random train/test split of geo-referenced 1 Hz traces, a protocol in which neighbours of
every test sample remain in the training set. Using a public dataset of 46,306 samples, we reproduce a
representative gradient-boosting study and audit how accuracy depends on three rarely varied factors. First,
protocol: the same model yields 0.97, 1.05 and 1.35 dB under a random hold-out, spatio-temporal patch-
based cross-validation with an embargo, and leave-one-group-out, respectively; protocol choice moves the
estimate by 0.39 dB, while the spread among six regressors collapses from 0.15 to 0.01 dB once the split
is blocked. Second, band information: adding the reported EARFCN (E-UTRA absolute radio frequency
channel number) reduces MAE from 1.05 to 0.73 dB and raises the weakest-band R2 from −0.41 to 0.65,
both under the mixed labelling of the released data; the gain depends on the label definition and ranges
from 15% to 31%. Third, device calibration: input-space recalibration of the same handset is absorbed by
the tree ensemble, whereas a 2 dB inter-device offset degrades MAE by 30% and a 100-sample output
calibration recovers 65–75% of that loss. Under the strictest protocol the band-aware model still beats the
reported random-split result by 10% (25% under spatio-temporal blocking). We conclude that protocol, band
information and device identity must be reported together, and give recommendations for crowdsourced
deployment.

I. INTRODUCTION
C
ROWDSOURCED monitoring of radio-frequency elec-
tromagnetic fields (EMF) promises a coverage and a
cost that fixed monitoring stations cannot match [1]–[3].
Personal exposure varies strongly in space and time [4] and
systematically between population groups [5], so the density
of a campaign matters as much as the accuracy of each
sample [6], and population-scale campaigns have been run
with phone-based instruments [7]. Regulatory frameworks
such as the ICNIRP guidelines [8] and IEEE C95.1 [9] define
exposure limits in terms of the electric field strength E, which
a smartphone cannot measure directly: the handset reports
downlink quality indicators, most notably the reference signal
received power (RSRP), and a calibration model must map
them to E.
Early approaches used a fixed conversion factor derived
from standardized extrapolation procedures [10] and were
limited to roughly 5 dB of accuracy in complex propagation
environments. Subsequent work replaced the fixed factor with
a learned mapping, and phones have served as exposure
sensors with kriging interpolation [11] and as subjects of
their own accuracy assessment [12], and software-modified
handsets have been used to validate self-reported use [13] and
to estimate transmitted power density [14]. Zeng et al. [15]
trained a gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) [16]–[18]
on 23 features derived from a seven-sample RSRP window
and reported a coefficient of determination R2 = 0.93 and
MAE = 0.97 dB, an improvement of about 4 dB over the

fixed factor. More recently, Huang et al. [19] fused RSRP,
RSSI, RSRQ and SINR in a stacking ensemble and reached
0.71 dB, attributing the gain to the complementary informa-
tion carried by the additional parameters.
Both results were obtained with a random 80/20 split of
data acquired by walking a route at 1 Hz. Consecutive samples
are then about 1.3 m and 1 s apart, so the neighbours of a
test sample almost certainly remain in the training set. This
inflates accuracy in exactly the regime that a crowdsourcing
deployment must handle, namely predicting at locations and
times that were never measured. Spatial and temporal block-
ing are standard practice in geostatistics and remote sensing
[20]–[24], but have not, to our knowledge, been applied to
smartphone-based EMF inversion, and the magnitude of the
resulting bias is unknown. The practice is not uncontested
[25], but where the target is prediction at locations that were
never visited, the blocked estimate is the one that matches the
use case [26].
A second observation motivates this work. The handset
knows which carrier frequency it is receiving: the E-UTRA
absolute radio frequency channel number (EARFCN) is re-
ported by the modem and is available at inference time. The
baseline model ignores it, although the four measured bands
differ by 12.4 dB in mean field strength and are even labelled
with different statistics. Exploiting a quantity that is already
available is free, and we quantify what it is worth.
This paper makes four contributions.
1) We reproduce a representative single-parameter EMF
inversion model on its public dataset to within 0.01
in R2 and 0.02 dB in MAE, and recover implemen-
tation details that the original paper does not report
(Appendix A).
2) We quantify how much of the reported accuracy is an
artefact of the evaluation protocol, using three protocols
of increasing strictness, and show that protocol choice
dominates model choice. We further show that R2 is
nearly insensitive to leakage, and that per-fold R2 be-
comes meaningless when a fold contains a single band,
because the internal variance is too small.
3) We show that a single EARFCN feature recovers most
of the accuracy that a band-agnostic model loses, and
that a per-band model gives no further gain.
4) We show that input-space recalibration of the same
device is absorbed by the model, whereas cross-device
calibration matters, at least in simulation, and we quan-
tify how many labelled samples it requires.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Sec-
tion II describes the dataset and the reproduction. Section III
defines the three protocols. Section IV reports the results
and ranks the factors. Section V treats device calibration.
Section VI discusses implications and Section VII concludes.

B. FEATURE CONSTRUCTION
For each field-strength timestamp, the RSRP series is cleaned
at −110 dBm, smoothed by an exponentially weighted mov-
ing average (EWMA) and resampled on a 1 s grid with
linear interpolation of gaps shorter than 5 s. A seven-point
window (t −3, . . . , t + 3) then yields 16 statistics—mean,
minimum, maximum, median, first and third quartiles, range,
variance, standard deviation, mode, skewness, kurtosis, mean
and median absolute deviation, interquartile range and coef-
ficient of variation—to which the seven raw RSRP values are
appended, giving 23 features. The label is the field strength
measured by the spectrum analyzer, taken as the third quartile
for EARFCN 100 and as the maximum for the other three
bands.
C. REPRODUCTION
We trained the published configuration (Huber loss with δ =
0.9, learning rate 0.05, maximum depth 5, 200 trees, 80/20
split) on the released sample table. Table 1 shows that all four
metrics match the published values to within 0.02 dB and 0.01
in R2.
Rebuilding the whole feature table from the raw measure-
ments reproduces the released seven raw RSRP values exactly
(MAE = 0) and the positional statistics with a correlation of
0.988, which confirms that the pipeline of Section II-B is the
one that generated the released samples. Details that had to
be inferred are listed in Appendix A.

B. SPATIO-TEMPORAL PATCH-BASED CV WITH EMBARGO
(ST-PB-CV)
The area is divided into 50 m patches and the timeline into
10 min blocks; the group is the (patch, block) pair, and groups
are assigned to five folds. An embargo removes from the
training set any sample lying within 20 m and 60 s of a
test sample, suppressing residual autocorrelation across block
boundaries. The block size is matched to the physics of the
features: 50 m is about 40 s of walking, comparable to the 3 s
window and the ≈20 s EWMA time constant of the α = 0.05
filter.
C. LEAVE-ONE-GROUP-OUT CV (LOGO-CV)
The group is the (campaign, band) pair, giving ten folds.
Holding out one group removes an entire measurement ses-
sion of one band, which mimics onboarding a new campaign
without recalibration. Grouping by campaign alone would
be incorrect here: each campaign measured only two of the
four bands, so a campaign-level hold-out would simultane-
ously remove two bands from training and confound cross-
session with cross-band generalisation. We verified that the
campaign-level variant yields 1.33 dB, comparable to the
(campaign, band) variant (1.35 dB) reported below.
D. METRICS
We report MAE, MSE (mean squared error) and RMSE (root
mean squared error), and a pooled R2 computed once over the
concatenated test predictions of all folds. Per-fold R2 is not
comparable across folds when the folds have different label
variance, as shown in Section IV-B.

B. WHY THE COEFFICIENT OF DETERMINATION
MISLEADS
Pooled R2 barely changes across protocols (0.934, 0.924
and 0.886) while MAE changes by 40%. The reason is that
R2 is dominated by the between-band spread of the field
strength, which blocking does not remove. Fig. 4 shows the
two mechanisms at work. Under the random split the training
and test CDFs of the field strength are indistinguishable
(Kolmogorov–Smirnov D = 0.012, p = 0.27): the marginals
match exactly, so a distributional check cannot detect the
leakage, which resides in the joint spatio-temporal structure.
Under LOGO-CV the ten (campaign, band) groups each oc-
cupy a narrow slice of the 13.3 dB spanned by their means,
so a held-out group is internally quiet (σ = 0.73–2.81 dB).
An error of 1.4 dB then produces a negative per-fold R2: the
values range from −2.62 to 0.61 with a mean of −0.18, even
though the MAE is unremarkable. Pooling the predictions
restores a meaningful value (0.886). We therefore recommend
that this literature report MAE and RMSE, and pooled R2 if
a coefficient of determination is required.

D. MODEL CHOICE
To place the protocol effect in context we compared six
regressors, five tree ensembles and a ridge baseline, under an
identical tuning budget (Table 6): random forests [28]–[30],
XGBoost [31] and LightGBM [32]. Under RH the ranking
is wide: Extra trees beats the published GBDT configuration
by 0.22 dB and the five ensembles span 0.15 dB. Under
ST-PB-CV the same models with the same hyperparameters
collapse onto each other: the five ensembles span 0.012 dB,
their pooled R2 lie within 0.002 of each other, and none is dis-
tinguishable from the published configuration. The advantage
of Extra trees is therefore itself a leakage artefact—a low-bias
learner benefits most from having near-duplicates of the test
points in the training set—and model choice, a 0.15 dB effect
under RH, is negligible once the split is blocked.
E. INTERACTION BETWEEN PROTOCOL AND FEATURES
The three effects are not additive. Table 4 decomposes them:
the EARFCN gain grows from 0.30 dB under RH to 0.32 dB
under ST-PB-CV and 0.48 dB under LOGO-CV, while the
penalty for the strictest protocol shrinks from 0.38 dB for the
band-agnostic model to 0.20 dB once the band is known. Part
of what looks like a protocol penalty is therefore a model that
does not know which band it is predicting, and at LOGO-
CV the band gain exceeds the entire baseline protocol effect.
The two factors are comparable in magnitude and entangled;
model choice is second-order under the random split (0.15–
0.22 dB) and negligible under blocking (0.01 dB). Reporting
a model without stating either the protocol or the feature
set is consequently uninformative. The band gains above are
measured under the mixed labelling of the released data, for
the reason given in Section IV-C.

A. INTER-DEVICE OFFSET
To estimate the impact we add a constant offset δ to the RSRP
features of the test set (positional features shift, dispersion
features scale, and the coefficient of variation is recomputed)
and evaluate under ST-PB-CV. Table 7 shows that a 2 dB
offset increases MAE by 30% and a 5 dB offset by 148%. A
100-sample output-bias calibration, estimated per band from
labelled samples disjoint from the test folds, recovers 65–75%
of the loss. At δ = 0 the calibrated model is 0.002 dB worse,
which is estimation noise from the 100 calibration samples.
B. WHY SAME-DEVICE INPUT CALIBRATION DOES NOT
WORK
A natural proposal is to characterise the radio-frequency re-
sponse of a given handset—antenna gain versus frequency,
front-end linearity, analogue-to-digital conversion bias—and
to correct RSRP before inversion. We tested this directly:
applying a fixed affine correction to all RSRP features, con-
sistently to training and test data, changes MAE from 1.0464
to 1.0458 dB, a difference of −0.001 dB. The reason is
structural: a tree ensemble compares feature values only, so it
is invariant to any monotone per-feature transformation, and
the correction is absorbed by the model.
Calibration therefore pays off only across devices, where
the offset is a genuine distribution shift that the model has
never seen, and only for the part of the response that is not a
monotone transformation—for example front-end compres-
sion at high or low levels, or noise that a correction can
actually remove.

C. EVALUATION DISCIPLINE
A calibration procedure must not be fitted on the data used
for evaluation. We quantified the effect: estimating the per-
band output bias from the test fold itself yields 0.972 dB,
whereas estimating it from disjoint labelled samples yields
0.984 dB. The 0.012 dB difference is the leakage in this
particular setting, and it grows with the number of calibration
parameters. We recommend either a separate calibration split,
or nesting the calibration inside each training fold.
VI. DISCUSSION
A. WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN FOR CROWDSOURCING
The three protocols answer different deployment questions:
interpolation between known neighbours, prediction where
nothing was measured, and reuse in a new campaign. The last
two matter for crowdsourcing; their MAE is 1.05 and 1.35 dB.
B. RECOMMENDATIONS FOR REPORTING
We suggest that future work (i) reports at least one blocked
protocol besides the random split, (ii) states the feature set,
in particular whether the band is used, (iii) reports MAE and
RMSE with a pooled R2, and (iv) states the handset model
and any cross-device calibration.
C. ERROR BUDGET AND COMPLIANCE
The absolute accuracy has to be read against the use it is
put to. Compliance assessment under IEC 62232 requires
an explicit measurement-uncertainty budget [33]; for a cal-
ibrated broadband probe in the field that budget is of the
order of 2 dB (k = 2), so an inversion error below it does
not by itself degrade a compliance decision. The mean field
strengths here (87–99 dBµV/m, i.e. 0.02–0.09 V/m) lie 55–
70 dB below the ICNIRP reference level of 61 V/m at 2 GHz
[8], so in this environment neither 0.97 nor 1.35 dB can
change a verdict. What the error does determine is whether
phone-based estimates support exposure mapping and trend
detection, and there the difference is material: the 12.4 dB
contrast between band means is 13 times the MAE under the
random split but only 9 times under LOGO-CV.
D. LIMITATIONS
All measurements come from a single handset and a single
campus area of 380 × 260 m, so the cross-device results of
Section V are obtained by simulation rather than by mea-
surement, and the conclusions may not transfer to indoor or
dense-urban environments. The dataset contains only RSRP,
so we cannot test whether the gain obtained from EARFCN is
complementary to, or overlapping with, the gain reported for
multi-parameter inputs [19]. Finally, widening the embargo
from 0 m/0 s to 20 m/60 s and then to 50 m/300 s increases
MAE from 1.03 to 1.05 and 1.07 dB, so the residual leakage
across the 20 m/60 s boundary is small.
VII. CONCLUSION
On
a
public
dataset
we
reproduced
a
representative
smartphone-based EMF inversion model and then varied
three factors that are usually fixed by convention. The eval-
uation protocol is the most influential: random splitting un-
derstates the error by 8% relative to spatio-temporal blocking
and by 40% relative to holding out a whole campaign. Band
information, which the handset already provides, is the cheap-
est genuine improvement, worth 15–31% depending on how
the label is defined. Device calibration matters only across
devices, at least in simulation, and a few hundred labelled
samples suffice; under the strictest protocol the band-aware
model still beats the reported random-split accuracy by 10%.
We therefore suggest that protocol, feature set and device
identity be reported together.
APPENDIX A RECOVERING UNREPORTED
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Three details absent from [15] had to be recovered from the
released sample table.
1) EWMA coefficient. Fitting the released features gives
α = 0.05; values in the range 0.03–0.10 reproduce the
released statistics to within 1 dB.
2) Quantile definition. The quartiles are order statistics at
positions (N + 1)/4, (N + 1)/2 and 3(N + 1)/4 of the
seven-point window, not linearly interpolated quantiles.
3) Window composition. The seven appended raw values
are unfiltered linear interpolations on the 1 s grid,
whereas the 16 statistics are computed on the EWMA-
filtered series.
With these choices the reconstructed feature table matches
the released one exactly for the seven raw values and with
a correlation of 0.988 for the positional statistics.
